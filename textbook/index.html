Phonological acquisition: Children’s learning of the sound patterns of language

Phonological acquisition: Children’s learning of the sound patterns of language

Mitsuhiko Ota

2026-09-04

Preface

This book is written primarily for upper-undergraduate and (post-)graduate students who are interested in learning how children acquire the sound patterns of language. It assumes only a basic knowledge of phonetics and phonology, and provides necessary background information throughout to ensure accessibility for students from various disciplines, including linguistics, speech sciences, psychology, and cognitive science. I also wanted the volume to serve as a useful resource for researchers seeking a survey of research on specific areas in phonological development and acquisition. For that reason, I made sure that the book is more extensively referenced than what is usually expected for an introduction.

This book is slightly different from some of the similarly titled books that are currently available. First, it does not subscribe to one particular theoretical perspective to understand and explain the process of phonological acquisition. Rather, it concentrates on presenting observations about how different aspects of phonology develop in a child and then explores different ways to explain why things happen that way. I have done this in order to avoid the trap of primarily looking at data that are directly relevant to the framework of choice. My judgment is that it is more useful to have a broader empirical coverage at the expense of theoretical unity. Second, I have striven to include studies based on both perception/recognition and production, two main sources of evidence related to phonological development. Traditionally, researchers with a background in psychology tended to focus on perception and recognition data, and those coming from linguistics on production data. This is no longer the case in research on the subject, but introductory materials on phonological acquisition still have a tendency to emphasize one over the other or to discuss them in separate chapters. In this volume, both types of data are addressed within the same chapter in order to gain a comprehensive understanding of a particular aspect of development from the perspective of perception/recognition and production. The only exception to this is the discussion of how word forms are acquired, which had to be split into two chapters (one on word recognition and another on word production) to keep the lengths of the chapters comparable.

It is impossible to cover everything that is known about the acquisition of phonology in a single volume. To keep things tractable, I have made the decision to concentrate on how spoken language phonology is learned by typically developing children. This means that I have had to leave aside two topics that should in theory be included in a book about phonological learning.

The first topic that lies outside the scope of the book is the development of phonology in children who do not follow the most common trajectories observed in the general population. Such atypical patterns of development may be found in children with physiological conditions (e.g., cleft lip and palate, orofacial myofunctional disorders, hearing loss), brain injuries, conditions specific to speech production (e.g., childhood apraxia of speech, dysarthria), broader genetic conditions (e.g., Down syndrome, fragile X syndrome) or neurodiversity (autism).

The second topic that is not discussed in this book is the phonological development of sign language. Some readers may be surprised to hear the term phonology used in conjunction with sign language. It is indeed the case that phonology is usually understood to mean the study of sound patterns in language. However, in linguistics, it can also be construed as the component of language that determines how meaning is encoded by means of physical signals, including both auditory and visual ones. In that sense, both spoken language and sign language have a phonological component, altough in different modalities. In spoken language, words and sentences are realized as patterns of sounds produced by the vocal tract, whereas in sign language, they are realized as visual gestures, which, in turn, are combinations of the shape, location, and movement of the hands and other body parts.

Fortunately, atypical phonological development is an area where a range of introductory volumes are on offer—too many to mention individually. A growing number of accessible overviews are also becoming available for the acquisition of sign language phonology (see, for example, the relevant chapter in Brentari (2019) and the video textbook by Pichler et al. (2018)). I refer the interested reader to these resources.

Ideally, a general account of phonological acquisition should be based on findings from a wide range of languages. A developmental observation about a handful of languages may not apply to others; and an account that works for typologically similar languages may not generalize to others. Sadly, our current understanding of phonological acquisition has been built on a rather limited number of languages. In particular, there is a noticeable skew toward English. This lack of linguistic diversity is part of a larger issue in language acquisition/development research. According to a survey based on articles featured in the four major journals on language development between 1974 and 2020, publications in the field covered 103 languages, which correspond to only about 1.5% of the approximately 7000 languages currently known to exist (Kidd & Garcia, 2022). More than half of those articles (53%) were about English, a proportion that was fairly constant across sub-areas, including phonology.

There is one advantage to this otherwise undesirable situation. Anyone who is reading this book can be assumed to be familiar with English, which is also the language for which the most amount of research is available. The strategy I have adopted is to exploit this state of affairs and use examples in English to describe and explain concepts, observations, and general issues as a starting point. I then refer to similar findings from as wide a range of languages as possible as a way to gauge the extent to which what is known about English-learning children can be extended to other languages. An obvious exception to this is when the phonological feature of interest (e.g., lexical tone) is missing in English, where other languages will be used to initiate the discussion.

Another caveat I need to mention is that much of the work discussed here has been carried out on the learning of one language, as opposed to two or more. This does not necessarily mean that all the infants and children featured in those studies were growing up in an exclusively monolingual environment. It is just that the majority of studies in the literature investigate the development of one language or one of the two or more languages to which their participants are exposed. Because of this, in most of the chapters, I will be making references to “the native language” or “the ambient language” in a singular form, while acknowledging that many children in the world hear more than one language around them. Work that specifically addresses bilingual/multilingual exposure will be discussed in Chapter 9.
